Hierarchical Reward Design from Language: Enhancing Alignment of Agent Behavior with Human Specifications

Rice University
Paper arXiv BibTeX

Figure 1. Unlike prior work on reward design, HRDL decomposes reward design into a low-level and a high-level reward. Language to Hierarchical Rewards (L2HR), our solution to HRDL, uses language models to synthesize these hierarchical rewards, enabling existing RL algorithms to train agents that are better aligned with human specifications.

TL;DR: Long-horizon behavior specifications are about what subtasks an agent does, in which order, and how. We formalize reward design for hierarchical RL agents and show that LLM-generated hierarchical rewards align agents with language specifications far better than flat rewards.

Abstract

When training AI agents to perform tasks, humans often care not only about whether a task is completed but also how it is performed. As agents tackle increasingly complex tasks, aligning their behavior with human-provided specifications becomes critical for responsible AI deployment. Reward design provides a direct channel for such alignment by translating human expectations into reward functions that guide reinforcement learning (RL). However, existing methods are often too limited to capture nuanced human preferences that arise in long-horizon tasks. Hence, we introduce Hierarchical Reward Design for Language (HRDL): a problem formulation that extends classical reward design to encode richer behavioral specifications for Hierarchical RL agents. We further propose Language to Hierarchical Rewards (L2HR), our proposed solution to HRDL. Human subject and numerical experiments show that Hierarchical RL agents trained with rewards designed via L2HR not only complete tasks effectively but also better adhere to human specifications. Together, HRD and L2HR advance the research on human-aligned AI agents.

Why Hierarchical Reward Design?

Humans rarely think about tasks as monolithic goals. We break them into subtasks: “first prepare the ingredients, then cook, then serve.” Hierarchical RL mirrors this structure and lets agents solve increasingly long-horizon tasks, but reward design for hierarchical agents has remained largely unexplored.

What

Which subtasks to perform

“Collect and deliver all supplies in the environment.”

Which order

How subtasks are sequenced

“Group objects of the same type: deliver all supplies of one type before switching.”

How

How each subtask is executed

“Avoid yellow danger zones when delivering an object.”

Existing reward design methods encode such specifications in a single flat reward, which scores each action using only the current state. A flat reward cannot see which subtask the agent is pursuing or which one it just finished, so it cannot express preferences over subtask order or subtask-dependent execution. We show, both theoretically and empirically, that flat rewards are fundamentally limited in capturing specifications for long-horizon tasks.

Many prior works assume access to hierarchical rewards for training agents, but the problem of designing them has not been formally defined. Just as the classical Reward Design Problem grounded algorithmic reward design for flat RL, HRD provides a principled foundation for designing hierarchical reward structures.

Hierarchical Reward Design (HRD)

The key idea: let the reward see the same subtask structure that a hierarchical agent uses. HRD replaces one flat reward with two: a high-level reward for choosing subtasks and a low-level reward for carrying them out.

FlatPrior work state action Each action
High-levelHRD previous subtask state next subtask Which subtask to do next

Highlighted inputs are subtask information that a flat reward never sees.

Knowing the previous subtask

Rules about order

“Deliver all supplies of one type before switching.” Whether the next delivery is acceptable depends on what was delivered last. The high-level reward sees that; a flat reward has no record of it.

Knowing the current subtask

Rules that depend on the subtask

“Avoid the stool while going to pick up or drop off an egg.” The same step near the stool is fine during one subtask and penalized during another. The low-level reward knows which subtask the agent is on; a flat reward sees the position but not the purpose.

From language to rewards (HRDL)

HRDL starts from the rewards a task already has: one for finishing the task and one for finishing each subtask. It keeps those and adds an alignment reward at each level, generated from the user's written specification:

High-level reward = task reward + alignment reward for choosing subtasks
Low-level reward = subtask-completion reward + alignment reward for carrying them out

Highlighted terms are generated from the natural-language specification.

Keeping the original rewards means the agent still learns to finish the task, while the alignment rewards shape how it does so. Each level is a standard RL problem, so off-the-shelf algorithms train it: PPO for the low level and DQN-style methods for the high level.

The goal is the pair of rewards whose trained agent best follows the specification. That is only measured afterwards, with handcrafted ground-truth rewards or human ratings, so the rewards have to be right without that feedback during training.

Hierarchical rewards can express everything flat rewards can, and more.

A hierarchical reward can simply ignore its subtask inputs and act like a flat one. The reverse doesn't hold: some specifications about which subtask comes next, or how to act during a particular subtask, can't be written as a flat reward at all. The paper proves this formally (Properties 1 and 2, Appendix B.3).

L2HR: Language to Hierarchical Rewards

L2HR prompts an LLM to write executable high- and low-level reward functions directly from a natural-language specification, without any feedback during training on whether the agent follows it.

Prompt components

  1. Task description, including the approximate scale of the task reward. The task reward code itself is withheld so the LLM does not overfit to implementation details.
  2. Environment code context exposing the state and action spaces without leaking simulation internals.
  3. Subtask (option) and action spaces, with the semantic role of each, so the LLM separates high-level from low-level decisions.
  4. Behavior specification describing the desired behavior beyond task completion.
  5. Formatting and reward design tips, e.g., no new global variables, and balance designed rewards against the task reward.

Example (iTHOR)

Two-stage training

Stage 1 · Low level

The LLM writes several candidate low-level alignment rewards (8 in our experiments). Each one trains a low-level policy together with the subtask-completion reward. Only policies that still reliably finish their subtasks are kept.

Stage 2 · High level

The LLM writes candidate high-level alignment rewards. Each one trains a high-level policy together with the task reward, on top of a kept low-level policy. Reward pairs whose agents still complete the task are returned.

Candidates are filtered with lightweight checks: the code must compile and reference only the state, subtask, and action variables exposed in the prompt. In practice, at least one sample per batch passes and preserves task feasibility. The two-stage structure keeps the design modular and lets subtask policies be reused.

Domains

Three long-horizon, multi-subtask domains. In Rescue World and iTHOR, flat rewards cannot in principle capture every specification; in Kitchen they can. This contrast tests how well language becomes reward in both regimes.

Rescue World

Variant of RW4T · 407-dim state · 8 subtasks

Collect and deliver all supplies in a disaster-response map.

High-levelPersistence: deliver all supplies of one type before switching.
Low-levelSafety: avoid hazard zones while carrying supplies.

iTHOR

AI2-THOR kitchen · 30-dim state · 8 subtasks

Carry apples and eggs from the dining table to the sink.

High-levelDiversity: deliver a different item type than the previous one.
Low-levelAvoidance: stay away from the stool while picking up or delivering an egg.

Kitchen

Single-agent Overcooked · 699-dim state · 5 subtasks

Prepare a salad with lettuce, tomatoes, and onions.

High-levelChopping: follow a preferred order, e.g., tomatoes → onions → lettuce.
Low-levelFixed low-level policies; no low-level reward design.

Q1Is the generated code valid?

Figure 5. Syntax error rates for LLM-based reward generation (24 candidates per configuration).

Hierarchical generation has far lower error rates in all three domains. Without access to the previous subtask, flat generation hallucinates variables such as last_delivered_type to enforce persistence; in Kitchen it struggles with coordinate-level action logic. Seeing the subtasks lets L2HR reason about steps like “chop onion” directly.

Hier preserves task feasibility more often than Flat in every domain. Flat rewards lean on spurious heuristics, such as inferring the last-delivered item from the agent's location, and accumulate reward in unintended ways.

Hier leads on high-level alignment everywhere, because persistence (Rescue World) and diversity (iTHOR) depend on the previous subtask, which flat rewards cannot see. In iTHOR, Hier also wins on low-level alignment (−3.75 vs. −35.20): flat rewards only penalized the stool at the moment of picking or placing, since the agent's intent is hard to infer from state alone.

Kitchen shows that hierarchy helps even when flat rewards are theoretically sufficient. A flat reward could encode the chopping order, but only one flat candidate did so correctly. High-level abstractions make the specification easier for the LLM to implement.

Evaluation with Human Participants

Ground-truth rewards are rarely available in practice, so we ran an IRB-approved study in which people, rather than handcrafted rewards, judged how well each agent followed the specification.

Participants recruited on Prolific watched videos of Flat and Hier agents in Rescue World and Kitchen, without knowing which method produced each, and rated how well each behavior matched the written specification from 1 (least aligned) to 5 (most aligned). We analyzed 30 participants who passed attention checks, split evenly across the two domains.

Conclusion

HRD formulates a reward structure that is more expressive than flat rewards, plugs into existing decision-making frameworks and RL algorithms, and better encodes behavioral specifications for long-horizon tasks. L2HR, an initial solution, achieves considerably better or comparable results in both numerical and human evaluations.

Our experiments use complex but simulated domains; evaluating HRD in real-world robotics and interactive AI systems is a natural next step, as is richer reward generation with evolutionary optimization or human feedback. Human-agent alignment is hard, and HRD is meant to complement learning from demonstrations, rankings, and corrections rather than replace them.
